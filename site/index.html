The problem

A question from 1962

Pick an odd number k and walk down the list k·2+1, k·4+1, k·8+1, … For almost every k a prime turns up quickly. In 1960 Wacław Sierpiński proved that for some k it never does: every number in the list is composite, forever. Those k are Sierpiński numbers, and in 1962 John Selfridge found one: 78,557.

Is 78,557 the smallest? To prove it, every odd k below it needs a prime somewhere in its list. Most fell quickly. By 2002, seventeen were left, and the volunteer project Seventeen or Bust set out to find them. PrimeGrid later took up the search. Twelve have fallen. A prime of more than a million digits that settles one is a Colbert number, a whimsical nod to Stephen Colbert, and this project's namesake.

For numbers of this shape there is a one-calculation test, due to François Proth in 1878: with P=k·2n+1 and a suitable small a,

Pis prime⟺a(P−1)/2≡−1(modP)

In practice: square a 14-million-digit number, reduce it modulo P, and repeat almost 48 million times. Each squaring needs the one before, so a test can't be split across computers. The only way to finish sooner is to make each squaring faster.

Primes this large are rare. Across the five, there's about a one-in-five chance of a prime before n reaches 57.5 million, and on average one turns up every half a million tests. Nobody knows which test it will be. It could be the one on your computer.

The idea

An exact transform with no padding

1Multiplying with transforms

Huge numbers are multiplied with a fast Fourier transform (FFT): digits become "frequencies", the product becomes a pointwise multiplication, and the transform runs in a circle, so anything that overflows the top wraps round to the bottom. For Mersenne numbers 2p−1 that wrap-around is exactly "modulo P" (Crandall and Fagin, 1994). That's why Mersenne testing is so fast.

2The trouble with k

For k·2n+1, overflow should wrap round divided by k. Programs either double the transform so nothing wraps (exact, but twice the data: proth20, the existing GPU tester for these numbers), or fold k into floating-point weights (Percival, 2002; Woltman's gwnum, behind PrimeGrid's CPU tests). Those weights need double precision, which gaming GPUs run 32–64× slower than their whole-number arithmetic.

3colbert's step

Do Percival's weighted transform exactly, in clock arithmetic: pairs of numbers modulo the Mersenne prime q=261−1, multiplied like complex numbers (the field GF(q2)). The weights need a root θ of a number that depends on k and n:

θN=−k·2nin GF(q2)

In most number systems that root doesn't exist for most k, which is why exact GPU testers were limited to special forms. In this one it always exists.

Why the root always exists

Every nonzero number modulo q is a sum of two squares, a2+b2, which in GF(q2) is a pair h times its mirror image h‾. Raising a pair to the power q mirrors it, so hq+1=h·h‾. And because q is a Mersenne prime, q+1=261 is a power of two, so it splits into N equal steps for any power-of-two transform length N:

h·h‾=a2+b2=−k·2nθ=h(q+1)/NθN=hq+1=h·h‾

θ is a pair of whole numbers, computed in a fraction of a second before each test, whatever k and n are. With the weights 2sj·θ−j, multiplying modulo P becomes one cyclic convolution of length N: no padding, and no rounding anywhere.

What's new, and what isn't

The ingredients are decades old: the weighted transform (Crandall and Fagin, 1994), this field and its transforms (Crandall, 1999), Percival's method for k (2002), and Woltman's and Gallot's work on practical testers. The new part is noticing that in GF(q2) over a Mersenne prime the roots always exist, so the pieces fit.

“Surprisingly no fast algorithm exists for k·bn +/- 1”

Yves Gallot, author of proth20 and genefer, on using GPU-friendly transforms for these numbers (PrimeGrid forum, 2024)

As far as we can find, colbert is the first. The prior-art review sets out the evidence and its limits, and the design notes have the proofs.

Exact by proof

Two fields, 261−1 and 231−1, combine to 92 bits of exact arithmetic. Before every run a bound (Cauchy–Schwarz over the actual word sizes) proves no value can overflow. There's no round-off to monitor.

Checked as it runs

A Gerbicz–Li check verifies the chain of squarings as it goes. A flipped bit from heat or a cosmic ray is caught, and the test rewinds to the last verified point. The test suite flips bits on purpose to prove it works.

Built for GPUs

In this field an eighth root of unity is (1+i)·230, so multiplying by it is an addition and a bit rotation. colbert's four-step transform does a squaring in three passes over each field's data. It's written in OpenCL, which NVIDIA, AMD, Intel and Apple graphics all support.

Speed

Less data per squaring, so faster tests

Measured

One leading-edge test in about 40 hours, down from 72 with colbert's first GPU kernels. The same laptop's 14 CPU cores take about 11.5 days.

Modelled

A desktop NVIDIA card

For an RTX 4090 the performance model gives tuned colbert 7–9 tests a day, against about 3.6 for proth20, and two to three times the tests per kilowatt-hour of today's CPU testing. The model and its caveats.

Nobody has run colbert on NVIDIA or AMD hardware yet. If you have one, your first test produces the first real numbers.

And less work to do

13,713 candidates removed by a deeper sieve, with every prime up to 232. Each one's factor is published, so anyone can check it.

1 in 4 remaining candidates has a factor that P−1 factoring finds for 3.7% of the cost of a test. The app tries it first, cutting the project's work by about 22%.

Join

Lend your computer to the search

  1. Download and open. The engine is included; there's nothing else to install.
  2. Give a name. It shows on the progress board. That, your computer's name and model, and your results are all the project sees.
  3. Let it run. It checks your graphics card and CPU against exact arithmetic, then tests numbers. Pause any time; progress is saved.
Mac Apple Silicon or Intel · macOS 12 or newer Colbert-macOS.dmg Drag Colbert to Applications and open it. The first time, macOS asks you to allow it: System Settings → Privacy & Security → Open Anyway. Windows Windows 10 or 11 · 64-bit Colbert-Windows-Setup.exe Installs for you only, no administrator rights. If SmartScreen appears: More info → Run anyway. Linux x86-64 · any recent distribution Colbert-Linux-x64.tar.gz Unpack and run ./Colbert. Graphics cards need their OpenCL driver; servers can run it headless.

The app opens in your web browser. It isn't code-signed yet, hence the one-time step above. All releases · How the app works · Build from source

Results are checked. Each device self-tests against GMP before it gets work. Results from new computers, and a random share of the rest, are re-tested on other computers, and a prime is confirmed independently before anyone announces it.

Any computer helps. A graphics card makes it fast. Without one, colbert uses the CPU; that's slower, but every finished test settles a number for good.
